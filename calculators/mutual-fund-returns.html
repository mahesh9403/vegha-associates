What this calculator computes

This is a backward looking tool. Instead of projecting a future corpus, it measures the return you have already earned. You enter the amount originally invested, the current or final value of the holding and the period held in years. The calculator returns two numbers: the absolute return, which is the total percentage growth, and the compounded annual growth rate, which is the smoothed yearly rate that would have produced the same result.

The formula in words

Absolute return compares the gain against what you put in. CAGR takes the ratio of final value to initial value, takes the root corresponding to the number of years held, and converts the result into a percentage.

Absolute return (percent) = ((Final value - Initial value) / Initial value) x 100

CAGR (percent) = (((Final value / Initial value)^(1 / n)) - 1) x 100

Here n is the holding period in years, which can be a fraction. For a holding of eighteen months, n is 1.5.

How to read the results

  • For any holding shorter than a year, quote the absolute return only. Annualising a three month return produces a flattering and misleading figure.
  • For anything longer than a year, CAGR is the number to use when comparing schemes, because it puts investments of different durations on a common footing.
  • Absolute return and CAGR always tell a consistent story but feel very different. A hundred percent absolute return sounds spectacular until you notice it took twelve years, which works out to a modest annual rate.

Practical points for Indian investors

  • CAGR only works cleanly for a single investment and a single redemption. If you invested through a SIP or made multiple purchases and withdrawals, the correct measure is XIRR, which weights every cash flow by the date it occurred. Your consolidated account statement from the registrars will usually show XIRR for you.
  • Compare returns against the scheme benchmark and its category, not against a different asset class. A debt fund is not underperforming because an equity index did better.
  • The return shown here is pre tax. To know what you actually kept, deduct capital gains tax at the rate applicable to the scheme category and holding period, and any exit load charged on redemption. For a fair comparison with a fixed deposit, always compare post tax against post tax.

Answers

Frequently asked questions

When should I use XIRR instead of CAGR?

Use CAGR when there was one investment and one final value. Use XIRR whenever money went in or came out at multiple dates, which covers every SIP, every top up and every partial redemption. XIRR discounts each cash flow by the time it was actually invested, so it is the only accurate measure for a staggered investment.

Is a high CAGR over a short period a good signal?

Not on its own. Short period returns are heavily influenced by the market cycle and by the starting point chosen, and a single strong year can dominate a three year figure. Look at rolling returns across several periods, the consistency of the fund against its benchmark, and the expense ratio before drawing conclusions.

Do dividends or IDCW payouts affect this calculation?

Yes. If you hold an income distribution cum capital withdrawal plan, amounts already paid out are not reflected in the current net asset value, so entering only the present value understates your return. Either add the payouts received to the final value, or compare using the growth plan values.

Does this calculator account for tax and exit load?

No, it reports gross return. Capital gains tax depends on whether the scheme is equity oriented or otherwise and on your holding period, at the rates in force for the year of redemption. Some schemes also charge an exit load if units are redeemed within a specified period, which reduces the amount actually credited to you.
