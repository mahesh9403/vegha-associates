What this calculator computes

The car loan EMI calculator prices the borrowing on a vehicle purchase. You enter the amount financed (the on road price less your down payment and any exchange value), the annual rate of interest and the tenure in months or years. The tool returns the equated monthly instalment, the total interest payable, the total repayment and a schedule showing how the outstanding balance falls month by month.

The formula in words

The mechanics are identical to any other reducing balance loan. Convert the annual rate to a monthly rate, convert the tenure to a number of months, and solve for the fixed payment that clears interest and principal exactly at the end of the term.

EMI = P x r x (1 + r)^n / ((1 + r)^n - 1)

P is the amount financed, r is the annual rate of interest divided by 1200 and n is the number of monthly instalments.

How to read the results

  • Total interest on a car loan is the number that matters most, because a car is a depreciating asset. Every rupee of interest is spent on something losing value.
  • Compare EMIs across tenures before you sign. A five year loan looks affordable each month but adds materially to the total outgo compared with a three year loan on the same principal.
  • The outstanding balance column is useful if you plan to sell or exchange the vehicle mid term, since you will need to clear the balance and obtain a no objection certificate from the financier.

Practical points for Indian buyers

  • Watch for flat rate quotes. Some dealers and financiers quote a flat rate of interest computed on the original principal for the whole tenure. A flat rate is roughly equivalent to a much higher reducing balance rate, so always ask for the reducing balance rate or the annual percentage rate before comparing offers.
  • The on road price includes ex showroom price, GST and cess, registration, road tax, insurance and any accessories. Lenders usually finance a percentage of ex showroom or on road value, so the down payment you actually need is often larger than expected.
  • Add the running cost to your monthly plan: insurance renewal, servicing, fuel or charging and parking. The EMI is only part of what owning the vehicle costs.
  • If the car is used for business or profession, interest on the loan and depreciation on the vehicle may be claimable, subject to the provisions of the Income Tax Act and proper documentation. Keep the loan in the correct name from day one.

Answers

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a flat rate and a reducing balance rate?

A flat rate charges interest on the full original loan amount for the entire tenure, even though you are steadily repaying principal. A reducing balance rate charges interest only on the outstanding amount, which falls every month. A flat rate is therefore far more expensive than the same number quoted on a reducing balance, so insist on comparing like with like.

How much down payment should I make on a car loan?

Lenders commonly finance a large share of the ex showroom or on road value, leaving the balance to you. A bigger down payment lowers both the EMI and the total interest, and reduces the risk of owing more than the vehicle is worth in the early years. As a rule of thumb, put down as much as you can without draining your emergency fund.

Can I claim tax benefit on a car loan?

A salaried individual buying a car for personal use gets no deduction for car loan interest. If the vehicle is used for business or profession, the interest and depreciation may be allowable as business expenditure under the Income Tax Act, subject to the extent of business use and adequate records. There are also specific provisions for electric vehicle loan interest, so check applicability for your year of borrowing.

Is it worth prepaying a car loan?

Often yes, since car loan rates are usually higher than home loan rates and there is no tax shield on personal use. Check the sanction letter first, because unlike floating rate home loans, car loans can carry foreclosure charges expressed as a percentage of the outstanding principal. Compare that charge against the interest you would save over the remaining tenure.
