What this calculator computes

A systematic investment plan puts a fixed amount into a mutual fund scheme at a fixed interval, usually monthly. This calculator projects where that discipline lands you. You enter the monthly instalment, an expected annual rate of return and the number of years. It returns the estimated maturity value, the total amount you will have invested and the estimated gains, which is simply the difference between the two.

The formula in words

Each instalment is a separate investment that compounds for a different length of time. The first instalment compounds for the full tenure, the last one for barely a month. Adding up all those future values gives the future value of an annuity.

FV = A x (((1 + i)^n - 1) / i) x (1 + i)

A is the monthly instalment, i is the monthly rate of return (the expected annual return divided by 1200) and n is the total number of instalments (years multiplied by twelve). The final term assumes the instalment is invested at the beginning of each month.

How to read the results

  • Look at the split between invested amount and gains. In the first few years your own money dominates. Somewhere beyond the tenth year the gains component typically overtakes the contributions, and that crossover is the whole argument for staying invested.
  • The expected return is an assumption, not a promise. Run the calculation at a conservative rate and at an optimistic rate to see the range you are actually planning within.
  • Extending the tenure moves the needle far more than increasing the instalment. Try adding five years before you try adding rupees.

Practical points for Indian investors

  • The projection assumes a steady rate of return. Real equity markets do not deliver evenly, so the actual corpus on any given date depends on where markets happen to be. Plan a glide path out of equity as a goal approaches rather than redeeming on the maturity date by default.
  • Consider a step up SIP that raises the instalment each year in line with your income. A modest annual increase has a disproportionate effect on the final corpus.
  • Returns shown here are before tax and before expenses. Capital gains on equity oriented and debt oriented schemes are taxed differently, and each SIP instalment has its own holding period for that purpose. The scheme expense ratio is already reflected in a fund net asset value, so use realistic return assumptions.

Answers

Frequently asked questions

What rate of return should I assume for a SIP?

There is no correct number, since returns are not guaranteed. Investors commonly model equity oriented funds conservatively and debt oriented funds lower still, but the honest approach is to test a range rather than fix on one figure. Use the calculator twice, once at a pessimistic rate and once at an optimistic rate, and plan against the lower outcome.

How is a SIP taxed in India?

Each instalment is treated as a separate purchase with its own holding period, and units are redeemed on a first in first out basis. Gains are taxed as short term or long term capital gains depending on that holding period and on whether the scheme is equity oriented or otherwise, at the rates in force for the relevant year. Equity linked savings schemes also carry a lock in and a deduction under the old regime, subject to the limits in the Act.

Can I stop or pause my SIP?

Yes. A SIP is not a contract to keep paying, and you can pause or cancel it with the fund house or platform, usually with a few working days notice before the next debit date. Units already allotted stay invested unless you redeem them. Missing an instalment does not attract a penalty from the fund, though your bank may levy a charge for a failed mandate.

Is a SIP better than a lumpsum investment?

A SIP suits money that arrives monthly from salary and it averages your purchase cost across market levels, which reduces the risk of investing everything at a peak. A lumpsum suits money already in hand and historically does better when markets rise steadily after the investment date. Many investors sensibly do both: a lumpsum for windfalls and a SIP for regular income.
